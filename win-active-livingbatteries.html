The Deep Ocean's Living Batteries

Unit 1: Active Comprehension • Central Idea & Tracking

[¶1] Thousands of meters beneath the ocean's surface, sunlight cannot penetrate the pitch-black water. In this freezing environment, towering chimneys called hydrothermal vents spew mineral-rich fluids directly into the sea from deep within the Earth's crust. For decades, marine biologists assumed life here could barely survive. Yet, thriving communities of giant tube worms, ghost-white crabs, and specialized shrimp crowd around these scalding chimneys.

[¶2] Instead of relying on photosynthesis like plants on land, these deep-sea organisms depend on a process called chemosynthesis. Microscopic bacteria absorb the toxic hydrogen sulfide and iron pouring from the vents, converting those dissolved minerals into usable chemical energy. The tube worms lack mouths and digestive systems; instead, billions of symbiotic bacteria live inside their bodies, feeding their hosts from the inside out.

[¶3] Recently, marine engineers made an even more unexpected discovery: these hydrothermal fields generate measurable electric currents. Because the hot vent fluids carry a heavy electrical charge compared to the cold surrounding seawater, the mineral walls of the chimneys act like natural power cables. Scientists are now testing whether this geological electricity could help explain how the earliest single-celled life formed on Earth.
